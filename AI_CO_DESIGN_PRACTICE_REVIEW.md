# Generative AI in design-thinking practice: 2024–2026 review

Date: 24 September 2026  
Status: accepted as the application's later-stage AI participation protocol; it does not claim a universal professional consensus.

## Executive finding

Generative AI has made design thinking more interesting chiefly by compressing the distance between a question and something the team can react to. It can rapidly produce alternative framings, provocations, rough prototypes, translations and critiques; it can also connect evidence across a long iteration history. This creates more opportunities to explore and learn within a workshop.

The same speed can cause premature convergence, fixation, homogenised ideas, fabricated research and loss of participant agency. Recent practice therefore points toward **configured co-creation**: make the AI's role, initiative, inputs, timing and authority explicit for each activity. Human participants may choose minimal use, selective delegation or close co-design. AI-free participation must remain a complete path through the product.

There is no single accepted “Design Thinking with GenAI” standard as of this review. The defensible approach is to combine recent co-creation research and practitioner patterns with recognised AI governance standards. This review proposes an app-level operating protocol for doing that.

## What changed in the last two years

### 1. From an empty canvas to rapid material for reflection

AI can turn a half-formed thought into multiple problem statements, scenarios, storyboards, prototype copy or interface alternatives in minutes. The benefit is not merely faster production: more ideas can become tangible enough to critique and test. IDEO's recent recommendation is to use AI's speed for more rough prototypes and more iterations, while retaining human learning-by-making and judgment ([IDEO, 2026](https://www.ideo.com/journal/rough-rapid-and-right-in-the-age-of-ai)).

Product implication: reward the number of assumptions tested and alternatives meaningfully compared, rather than the amount of generated material.

### 2. From a linear chatbot to a nonlinear design space

Designers branch, remix, compare and return to earlier ideas. A CHI 2024 study with design experts found that conventional linear prompting poorly matches that process and proposed a nonlinear human–AI co-design framework, implemented in OptiMuse ([Microsoft Research / CHI 2024](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/understanding-nonlinear-collaboration-between-human-and-ai-agents-a-co-design-framework-for-creative-design/)).

Product implication: keep alternatives side by side on the board, preserve branches and provenance, and let a team combine parts of several suggestions. Do not make a chat transcript the primary representation of co-design.

### 3. From one fixed AI role to negotiated agency

An ethnographic study of a NASA design team observed three legitimate strategies: intimate co-design, selective delegation and minimal AI use ([Ma et al., ASME 2024/NASA](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20240003050/downloads/Ma%20et%20al%20Human-AI%20Three%20Strategies.pdf)). The 2025 Co-Creative Design Framework similarly describes co-creation through agency, interaction dynamics and communication rather than assuming one ideal relationship ([Davis, Sherson and Rafner, C&C 2025](https://pure.au.dk/portal/en/publications/the-co-creative-design-framework-for-hybrid-intelligence/)).

Product implication: the team's AI mode is an explicit method setting, not a hidden product default. Users must be able to see who initiated a contribution and who retains the decision.

### 4. From ideation abundance to diversity management

AI can help an individual produce a stronger result, but shared model defaults can pull many people toward similar outputs. A 2024 *Science Advances* experiment found improved individual story evaluations alongside lower collective content diversity in its particular writing task ([Doshi and Hauser, 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11244532/)). A CHI 2024 experiment found increased design fixation when participants ideated with an image generator in its study setting ([Zhou and Lee, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1145/3613904.3642919)). Other studies report different effects under different exposure conditions, so “AI always reduces creativity” is not supported.

Product implication: collect human ideas before revealing AI examples when divergence matters; generate deliberately different mechanisms and perspectives; measure semantic spread, repeated patterns and neglected directions; separate generation from evaluation.

### 5. From synthetic users to AI-supported research operations

AI is useful for interview guides, translation, transcription, retrieval and provisional coding. It is not a substitute for the lived context, contradictions and unexpected cues of real research. IDEO explicitly argues against treating generated personas as research participants and recommends using AI to improve the research process instead ([IDEO, 2025](https://www.ideo.com/journal/the-case-against-ai-generated-users)). IDEO has also explored consented conversational tools to engage more people in framing questions at scale ([IDEO, 2025](https://edges.ideo.com/posts/qualitative-at-scale)).

Product implication: generated personas, simulated interviews and predicted reactions are labelled **hypotheses or rehearsal**, never evidence. The evidence store accepts only attributable human research or explicitly identified external sources.

### 6. From one-off generation to evidence-linked iteration memory

The compelling opportunity for this application is longitudinal: relate an observation to an insight, framing, prototype, test and later decision. AI can retrieve exceptions, compare checkpoints and challenge a conclusion when later evidence conflicts with it. The design contribution is the provenance and review flow around the model, not simply a chat endpoint.

Product implication: every accepted AI synthesis keeps references to the exact source revisions, preserves contradictory evidence, and becomes stale when its sources change.

## Recommended practice by design-thinking stage

| Stage | Current opportunity | Recommended collaboration pattern | What AI must not become |
|---|---|---|---|
| Understand | Generate alternative scopes, expose assumptions, broaden stakeholder questions | Human writes the initial challenge; AI offers competing framings and a devil's-advocate review | Authority on whose needs matter |
| Observe | Prepare questions, transcribe with consent, organize selected notes, find counterexamples | AI assists research operations and proposes evidence-linked themes for review | A synthetic research participant or source of invented quotes |
| Define | Compare insight statements and How might we framings against evidence | Human–AI proposal, critique and revision loop; show supporting and conflicting evidence | Final arbiter of the “correct” problem |
| Ideate | Produce analogies, distant mechanisms and visual provocations | Human-first silent round; optional AI expansion; later side-by-side evaluation | First voice in every brainstorm, voter or idea owner |
| Prototype | Create rough text, image and interactive alternatives quickly | Generate several deliberately rough variants tied to a learning question | A polish engine that disguises unresolved assumptions |
| Test | Draft neutral tasks, summarize observations, compare results with predictions | Humans conduct tests; AI performs provisional organization and flags missing evidence | Synthetic user evidence or automated proof of desirability |
| Reflect | Compare iterations, retrieve dissent and draft the learning narrative | AI prepares a traceable draft; team edits and records the decision | Rewriter of closed history or evaluator of participant performance |

This pattern alternates human intent, AI expansion, human critique, evidence checking and team decision. It avoids assigning every stage to a different autonomous agent.

## AI participation agreement

Every project establishes the maximum permitted mode. Every activity may use that mode or a more restrictive one. Increasing AI involvement during a live activity requires renewed acknowledgement from active participants whose contributions may be processed.

| Mode | AI behavior | Appropriate use |
|---|---|---|
| **AI-free** | No model receives activity content; ordinary templates, collaboration, voting and reports remain available | A team or participant declines AI; sensitive research; deliberate craft or learning exercises |
| **On-demand** | A person invokes help on content they are authorised to use; nothing runs in the background | Explanation, rewriting, individual provocations and small selected-note tasks |
| **Facilitated assist** | Facilitator starts a visible shared analysis over an agreed scope; team reviews before it enters the board | Theme proposals, assumption review, checkpoint synthesis and alternative framings |
| **Active co-design** | AI may offer bounded suggestions at agreed moments and may use approved background indexing | Experimental workshops with informed participants and an explicit turn-taking protocol |

The agreement records:

1. **Purpose:** what AI will help with and the intended benefit.
2. **Inputs:** which activities, attachments and contribution types may be processed; provider destination and retention.
3. **Initiative:** who can invoke AI, whether it may make unsolicited suggestions, and when it must yield.
4. **Timing:** human-first, AI-first, alternating, synthesis-only or critique-only.
5. **Authority:** AI may generate or recommend; it cannot vote, approve, close an iteration or represent stakeholder consent.
6. **Visibility:** which prompts, suggestions and accepted results are private, shared or included in the final report.
7. **Attribution:** AI origin, requesting person, model/prompt version, source references and human acceptance are retained.
8. **Challenge and exit:** users can inspect sources, edit, dismiss, undo, exclude their content and continue through an AI-free path.

For mixed-consent teams, a contributor can mark an item **Do not use with AI**. That item remains usable by people but is omitted from model context, embeddings, AI caches and derived summaries. The facilitator cannot override this restriction. If removing excluded material makes a synthesis misleading, the app should say that the available source set is incomplete rather than pretending to provide a team-wide view.

The interface should call the system an **AI assistant** or name the current function (“Evidence review”, “Idea provocations”). It may behave like a co-designer, but it is not counted as a participant, given a vote, listed as an author or allowed to blur human accountability.

## How recognised standards apply

These are governance standards and principles, not design-thinking methods:

- **NIST AI RMF and the July 2024 Generative AI Profile:** use Govern–Map–Measure–Manage to define roles, context, risk, testing, incident handling and third-party dependencies. NIST explicitly calls for differentiated human–AI responsibilities, documented knowledge limits and human oversight ([NIST AI RMF Core](https://airc.nist.gov/airmf-resources/airmf/5-sec-core/), [NIST AI 600-1](https://www.nist.gov/publications/artificial-intelligence-risk-management-framework-generative-artificial-intelligence)).
- **OECD AI Principles, updated May 2024:** ground product policy in human agency, privacy, fairness, transparency, robustness and accountability ([OECD overview](https://oecd.ai/en/ai-principles), [human-centred values](https://oecd.ai/en/dashboards/ai-principles/P6)).
- **ISO/IEC 42001:** useful when an organisation needs a formal AI management system covering accountability, impact/risk assessment, operation and improvement. Product conformance should not be claimed merely because this proposal borrows its themes ([ISO overview](https://committee.iso.org/artificial-intelligence/ai-management-systems)).
- **EU AI Act:** relevant deployments need legal review. The broadly useful product lesson is contextual AI literacy: providers and deployers must consider people's knowledge, experience and usage context ([official Regulation (EU) 2024/1689](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/ALL/?uri=CELEX%3A32024R1689)). A two-minute method-specific explanation is more useful than a generic disclaimer.
- **C2PA Content Credentials:** consider this for exported AI-generated images or media where interoperable provenance matters. Internal note provenance still requires application records. Content Credentials report origin/history; they do not prove that the content is true ([C2PA specification](https://spec.c2pa.org/specifications/specifications/2.2/specs/C2PA_Specification)).

For the app, NIST provides the risk lifecycle, OECD provides durable human-centred principles, ISO 42001 informs organisational governance, the EU AI Act informs literacy and transparency obligations, and C2PA is an export-level provenance option. None resolves workshop consent on its own; the AI participation agreement does that operationally.

## Required interface changes

### Before an activity

- Show the current mode beside the activity title: **AI free**, **On demand**, **Facilitated**, or **Active**.
- Open a short preflight card showing purpose, eligible content, provider/retention summary, timing and facilitator controls.
- Let each participant acknowledge the mode and exclude their contributions. Mode escalation waits for the affected active participants; lowering the mode is immediate.
- Provide a method-specific “What AI can and cannot do here” explanation.

### During an activity

- Show when AI is reading or generating and identify the exact selected scope.
- Preserve a human-only round and concealed contributions where the method calls for independent divergence.
- Place AI proposals in a review layer; do not add them to the shared board as ordinary notes automatically.
- Label evidence, interpretation, hypothesis and generated artifact distinctly.
- Keep source links, exceptions, requester and acceptance action on every shared suggestion.

### After an activity

- Include accepted AI contributions and rejected high-impact proposals in the activity record, without filling the report with routine requests.
- Let the team review whether AI narrowed the space, omitted a voice or changed the framing.
- Preserve decisions as human-owned and show the evidence available at that revision.
- Support deletion and retention rules across prompts, embeddings, caches and derived artifacts.

## Product rules to adopt

1. Complete functionality without AI is a product requirement, not a degraded fallback.
2. AI use is visible and scoped; no silent processing of workshop content.
3. Research evidence, human interpretation and AI-generated hypotheses remain separate data types.
4. The assistant does not impersonate users or stakeholders and cannot create research evidence.
5. Divergent work starts with independent human contributions by default; AI enters at a declared moment.
6. Suggestions are reversible and reviewed before they change shared records.
7. Accepted syntheses cite exact sources and surface contradictions, missing voices and stale inputs.
8. AI has no vote, facilitation authority, participant seat or ownership claim.
9. Per-contribution exclusion is enforced across inference, retrieval, caching and training/data-use settings.
10. Teams receive method-specific AI literacy and a debrief on AI influence.
11. Models and prompts are evaluated using representative activities for fidelity, diversity, bias, latency and cost.
12. The product makes no claim of ISO certification, legal compliance or research validity without the appropriate independent work.

## Evidence limits and next validation

The evidence base is recent and context-dependent. Studies use different creative tasks, models, participant expertise and measures; findings on fixation and diversity are not uniform. Practitioner articles indicate useful patterns but are not controlled evidence. Framework proliferation in 2025–2026 shows active exploration more than settled doctrine.

Validate the proposed protocol through paired workshops:

- AI-free versus facilitated-assist empathy-map synthesis;
- human-first versus AI-visible-from-start ideation;
- selective delegation versus active co-design for prototyping;
- teams with mixed AI preferences, including a participant who excludes their contributions.

Assess traceability to evidence, distinct useful directions, preservation of dissent, quality of learning questions, facilitator workload, participant agency and ability to explain AI's influence. Do not use output volume or AI acceptance rate as the primary outcome.
